\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented generation is often guarded by a faithfulness score, which decides whether the retrieved documents support an answer. The same scores are used to compare systems, so they settle both what a user sees and what a paper concludes. Such scores are ranked by how well they agree with human hallucination labels, or tested on reference summaries edited to contain one error. Neither shows whether a scorer treats answers from different generators alike.

We hold 32,000 answers fixed --- four embedding models, two question answering datasets, four generators --- and score every answer with three evaluators. We then inject a known error into up to 200 answers per system, dataset and generator, replacing one grounded value with a value absent from the retrieved context. Under a standard entailment scorer, a fixed threshold catches 5.1\% of these edits on the most verbose generator and 56.6\% on the tersest: one wrong value is largely diluted among the correct claims around it. Length is not the whole story, since at a matched single assertion the four generators still respond by between 0.102 and 0.485, and removing an opening ``according to the provided context'' clause from the answer closes part of that gap.

Comparing the four retrieval systems, whether that comparison returns a difference or a null depends on which evaluator scored it, in three of eight dataset-generator cells. The evaluators are not shown to rank the systems differently; they differ in how small a difference they can resolve. Averaging in refusals, or choosing a different equivalence margin, moves the conclusion as well. A faithfulness result should name its evaluator and test its threshold on deliberately falsified answers.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% Move 1: topic and why it matters
A document question answering system is given a set of uploaded files and must answer from them. If an invoice records a project budget of \$1,000, an answer stating \$1,500 fails in a specific way: fluent, on topic, citing the right document, wrong in the one place that matters. Such systems are typically guarded by a faithfulness score, which measures whether the retrieved text supports the answer \citep{maynez-etal-2020}. The same scores are used to compare systems \citep{es-etal-2024,tamber-etal-2025}, so they decide both what a user sees and what a paper concludes.

% Move 2: state of knowledge, and the gap it leaves
Many such scores ask a natural language inference model whether the retrieved context entails the answer, taking the maximum over retrieved chunks. Others use a trained alignment model \citep[AlignScore;][]{zha-etal-2023} or a language model as judge \citep{saad-falcon-etal-2024,tamber-etal-2025}. They are ranked by how well they agree with human hallucination labels \citep{honovich-etal-2022,tang-etal-2023,niu-etal-2024,bao-etal-2025}, or tested on reference summaries edited to contain one error \citep{ma-etal-2023}. Neither tells us whether a scorer can separate the systems someone is actually comparing, and pairs built from reference text cannot show whether a scorer treats answers from different generators alike.

% Move 3: aim and hypothesis
This paper asks what such a score measures. We do not propose a new one. We take 32,000 answers from a fixed grid --- four embedding models, two datasets \citep{kwiatkowski-etal-2019,yang-etal-2018}, four generators --- score every answer with three evaluators, and ask whether the verdict on embedding choice survives a change of evaluator. The grid is a testbed rather than the object of study: its retrieval systems span 4.4 and 11.9 NDCG@5 points on the two datasets while generation stays fixed. We expected that a score rating a whole answer at once would react less to one wrong value when more claims surround it, so its sensitivity would depend on the generator.

% Move 4: findings
Our main findings are:
\begin{itemize}
\item Whether comparing the four systems returns a difference or a null depends on which evaluator scored it, in three of eight dataset--generator cells after correcting for multiple tests. The evaluators are not shown to rank the systems differently; they differ in how small a difference they can resolve (Section~\ref{sec:verdict}).
\item Swapping one grounded number in an answer for a value absent from the context, a fixed threshold under the entailment scorer catches the edit in 5 to 14\% of the most verbose generator's answers and 57 to 71\% of the tersest's (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).
\item Making one model more verbose by prompt alone cuts that detection from 35.1\% to 10.4\%, and detection falls under all three evaluators. The same prompt line also lowers the refusal rate and raises the score an unrelated document receives, so it does not vary answer length alone (Section~\ref{sec:verbosity}).
\item Averaging in refusals, or choosing a different equivalence margin, changes the conclusion too (Sections~\ref{sec:population} and~\ref{sec:equivalence}).
\end{itemize}


\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Retrieval quality and downstream outcome.}
\citet{salemi-zamani-2024} find that relevance labels from human annotators or from an LLM correlate only weakly with downstream RAG performance, and propose eRAG, which labels each retrieved document by what the system's own generator produces from it alone. Their strongest baseline, answer-containment labelling, is the scheme our qrels use, and it keeps a moderate Kendall's $\tau$ of 0.31 to 0.40 on the question-answering datasets. Their correlations fall as $k$ grows and are reported at $k = 50$, against our 5, and their downstream measure is correctness from a fine-tuned 60M-parameter reader that cannot abstain. Ranking metrics may also miss how well the context covers what a long-form answer needs \citep{ju-etal-2025-controlled}, and in mathematics question answering different embedders succeed on different queries \citep{chen-etal-2025}. We take this gap as given and ask what a faithfulness evaluator can resolve between retrieval systems, for prompted generators that can refuse.

\paragraph{Faithfulness scorers.}
Surveys of RAG evaluation place faithfulness among the metrics for the generation component, beside relevance and accuracy \citep{yu-etal-2025-evaluation}. A systematic review of twelve RAG evaluation studies links each dimension, faithfulness included, to the metrics proposed for it and to the dataset and question types those metrics require \citep{knollmeyer-etal-2026}. \citet{laban-etal-2022-summac} showed that entailment models detect document-level inconsistency once the source is split into smaller premises and pairwise scores are aggregated; both entailment aggregates we test are of this kind (Section~\ref{sec:def-faith}). AlignScore \citep{zha-etal-2023} trains one alignment function across factual-consistency tasks, MiniCheck \citep{tang-etal-2024} trains small fact-checkers for grounding documents, and RAG frameworks add other dimensions or delegate the judgement to a language model \citep{es-etal-2024,saad-falcon-etal-2024,tamber-etal-2025,murugaraj-etal-2026}, which can itself be trained for the task \citep{li-etal-2025-rag}. Claim-level scoring verifies atomic claims one at a time \citep{min-etal-2023-factscore}, so its result depends on the decomposition method \citep{wanner-etal-2024-closer}, and decomposition trades accuracy gains against the noise it introduces \citep{hu-etal-2025-decomposition}. ReDeEP \citep{sun-etal-2025} traces hallucination to attention heads and feed-forward components. Faithfulness has also been separated from correctness on the generator side, for citations \citep{wallat-etal-2025} and for intermediate reasoning \citep{xu-etal-2025}. We propose no new scorer. We compare what three common ones detect on identical inputs, and \movedref~\ref{sec:beyond-nli} considers scorers we did not run.

\paragraph{Meta-evaluation of scorers.}
A separate line of work scores the detectors against human labels: TRUE \citep{honovich-etal-2022}, AGGREFACT \citep{tang-etal-2023}, RAGTruth \citep{niu-etal-2024}, FaithBench \citep{bao-etal-2025} and the multilingual MEMERAG \citep{cruz-blandon-etal-2025-memerag}. No single metric is best in all settings or for all error types, detection falls to about 50\% on summaries where current detectors disagree, and across 37 language models hallucination metrics often fail to align with human judgments \citep{kulkarni-etal-2025-evaluating}. \citet{chen-etal-2026-rethinking-evaluation} add a long-context RAG benchmark with realistic label noise. A second line constructs the errors. FactCC \citep{kryscinski-etal-2020-evaluating} trains on rule-based edits that include entity and number swaps, GO FIGURE \citep{gabriel-etal-2021-go} measures metric sensitivity on controlled diagnostic pairs, and BUMP \citep{ma-etal-2023} edits reference summaries into minimal pairs. GroUSE \citep{muller-etal-2025-grouse} unit-tests RAG judges on generator failure modes and finds agreement with \mbox{GPT-4} an incomplete proxy for passing them, and \citet{jing-etal-2025-scale} compare language-model and NLI scorers on synthetic unfaithful outputs. Our probe belongs to this second line, with two differences: the substituted value is absent from the retrieved context, while FactCC draws its replacements from the source document, and the edit is made to answers from the systems being compared, so each evaluator is tested on the population it would score.

\paragraph{Reliability of system comparisons.}
\citet{xiao-etal-2023} argue for treating generation metrics as measurement instruments with stated reliability and validity. In summarization, the system-level correlation of ROUGE with human judgments is close to zero when systems differ by the small margins common in practice \citep{deutsch-etal-2022-examining}. Length is a known confound: response-length heuristics rival complex hallucination detectors \citep{janiak-etal-2025-illusion}, and LLM judges favour longer responses in preference comparisons \citep{hu-etal-2025-explaining}. Refusals decide which answers get scored, and they depend on the system. No RAG configuration is best on both answerable and unanswerable queries across knowledge bases \citep{peng-etal-2025-unanswerability}, and even frontier models fall below 50\% refusal accuracy on multi-document tasks \citep{muhamed-etal-2026-refusalbench}. Our results concern all three, resolving power, answer length and the evaluation population, measured for RAG faithfulness on one fixed generation grid.

\section{Definitions}
\label{sec:defs}


\subsection{Faithfulness}
\label{sec:def-faith}

For a generated answer a and retrieved context R = \{d1, ..., dk\}, the standard aggregate is:

\begin{equation}
F_{\max}(a, R) \;=\; \max_{d \in R} \mathrm{NLI}(d \models a)
\end{equation}

We also evaluate a claim-level aggregate. Writing C(a) for the assertions in a:

\begin{equation}
F_{\mathrm{claim}}(a, R) \;=\; \min_{c \in C(a)} \; \max_{d \in R} \mathrm{NLI}(d \models c)
\end{equation}

Taking the maximum over chunks makes sense, since any one retrieved document may support a claim. Taking it over claims, which (1) does implicitly, does not. We therefore score an answer by its weakest assertion, so that one unsupported claim cannot sit hidden behind several supported ones. That is a design choice and not a definition of faithfulness. A minimum is the most severe aggregation available, and it is what drives claim-min's absolute scores toward zero as answers lengthen (Section~\ref{sec:verbosity}). An average over claims, or an aggregate weighted by how much content each claim carries, would encode a different and equally defensible view of what a partly supported answer is worth. We take the severe one deliberately and report what it costs. Throughout, faithfulness is measured against the generated answer and the retrieved context. The gold answer never enters it.

A natural summary would subtract faithfulness from NDCG@5 and report the gap. We do not use one, for two reasons. On Natural Questions the difference comes out negative for every model, because faithfulness (0.85) runs above retrieval quality (0.79), so the metric presumes a loss of fidelity that does not occur. And any metric of the form 1 - F/RQ reproduces the retrieval ranking whenever F is near-constant across systems, which it is here. We report absolute faithfulness instead, which has the further advantage of keeping the both-low case visible; a difference cannot tell it apart from both-high.


\subsection{Correctness and retrieval hit}
\label{sec:def-correct}

Correctness is measured against the gold answer, never against the context. Keeping the two apart is what lets a grounded wrong answer and an ungrounded right answer stay distinguishable. A retrieval hit means the gold answer string appears somewhere in the retrieved context. That is a strong definition of retrieval success, and Limitations sets out what it misses.


\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Two datasets are used, Natural Questions \citep{kwiatkowski-etal-2019} and HotpotQA \citep{yang-etal-2018}, at 1,000 queries each. Four embedding models index each corpus, and retrieval takes the top five chunks of 256 tokens with 32-token overlap. Four generators answer from a byte-identical prompt at temperature 0: two proprietary (Claude Haiku 4.5, GPT-4o-mini) and two open-weight run locally (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3). The two open-weight arms share a decoding path and differ only in weights, which is what makes them two generators rather than one model prompted twice. That gives 4 x 2 x 4 x 1,000 = 32,000 answers, each re-scored by every evaluator. The four systems are all-mpnet-base-v2 (contrastive), BGE-M3 (multilingual), E5-large-instruct (instruction-tuned) and text-embedding-3-small (proprietary). They reach NDCG@5 of 0.786, 0.794, 0.806 and 0.830 on Natural Questions and 0.705, 0.809, 0.824 and 0.767 on HotpotQA, a spread of 4.4 and 11.9 points. They do not rank the same way on the two datasets (Appendix~\ref{app:retrieval}). A single prompt builder enforces parity, one user turn and no system message, since a prompt difference would confound every cross-generator comparison below.

Four further arms run off the same grid, in \movedrefs~\ref{sec:control} to~\ref{sec:qasper}. A fifth embedding model, jina-embeddings-v3, was run over both datasets and all four generators. It serves as a control on the comparison and is not a member of the matched set. A third dataset, QASPER \citep{dasigi-etal-2021}, was run at 819 questions over all five embedding models and three of the four generators, Mistral-7B excepted. The floor, ceiling and context-arrangement conditions and the re-ranking analysis were run on the Claude arm; the geometric analysis uses no generator. Their scope is stated where they appear and again in the Limitations.

Three evaluators are compared, on identical inputs. NLI-max is (1) with a DeBERTa-v3-large \citep{he-etal-2023} inference model, a common choice in the work cited in Section~\ref{sec:related}. It uses the entailment probability alone. \citet{steen-etal-2023} show that an NLI scorer predicts faithfulness better when it also uses the contradiction probability, and better again with task-adapted training and Monte-Carlo dropout. We score the out-of-the-box configuration, since the question here is what published comparisons rest on, and we test the first of those changes directly. Re-scoring every falsification case as entailment minus contradiction (Appendix~\ref{app:ec}) leaves detection at the floor-anchored gate within two points of NLI-max in all eight dataset-generator cells. Claude stays the least detected generator and GPT-4o-mini the most, and AlignScore still has the larger mean drop in every cell. The contradiction term moves the scale, not what the scorer responds to. We did not try task-adapted training or Monte-Carlo dropout. Claim-min is (2) with the same model and sentence-level claim segmentation, chosen so no second generator enters the measurement loop. AlignScore \citep{zha-etal-2023} is an independently trained alignment model.

The answers are byte-identical across evaluators. The premises are not, and that difference is part of what the comparison measures, because premise construction follows each evaluator's intended interface. The entailment scorer sees each of the five chunks separately, plus their concatenation truncated to the scorer's own 512-token window, and takes the maximum. The claim-level scorer applies the same construction per extracted claim. AlignScore takes the concatenated context as one premise and splits it internally. Some of the difference in Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} is therefore premise granularity rather than evaluator sensitivity; the Limitations section bounds that share by re-scoring the same cases on the concatenated premise alone. Given AlignScore's own premise, the entailment model still responds less than AlignScore in all eight dataset-generator cells, by mean drop and by detection. The hypothesis is normalised identically for all three, with markdown emphasis, headings and list markers stripped before scoring. That matters because Claude emits markdown in 76.0\% of its attempted answers against 1.3\%, 0.3\% and 0.2\% for Qwen, GPT-4o-mini and Mistral-7B, and the entailment model responds to it (\movedref~\ref{sec:normalisation}).

One refusal rule, from a single shared implementation, produced every evaluator score, falsification case and comparison reported below, so those sections describe the same population. The one exception is the correctness analyses of \movedref~\ref{sec:refusals} and Appendix~\ref{app:cell}: where a judge grades correctness it also classifies refusals, and those analyses take its classification. \movedref~\ref{sec:graders} measures how far the two classifications differ, and Appendix~\ref{app:refusalrule} reports the headline count under this rule, its bare form and the judge. An answer counts as a refusal when, after SQuAD-style normalisation, it begins with one of a fixed list of declining phrases. The rule excludes 8,055 of the 32,000 answers (25.2\%), from 11.8\% on NQ/Qwen to 39.0\% on HotpotQA/Mistral-7B. The list is wider than the refusal sentence the prompt offers, because generators decline in their own words: Mistral-7B opens 378 refusals with ``The provided context does not''. A leading ``Based on the provided context,'' is stripped before the anchored test, since Claude prepends it to 97.5\% of its attempts and to some of its refusals. Without the wider list and the stripping, 759 refusals would count as attempts: 107 of Claude's, 50 of Qwen's, 602 of Mistral-7B's and none of GPT-4o-mini's.



\subsection{Terms used throughout}
\label{sec:terms}

Four words carry specific meanings below and are easy to read past.

A \textbf{cell} is one combination of the factors being held fixed, and the factors differ by analysis, so we always name the unit. A \emph{dataset-generator cell} is one of eight, a \emph{dataset-generator-evaluator cell} one of twenty-four, and a \emph{dataset-generator-system cell} one of thirty-two. Unqualified, ``cell'' means the first.

An \textbf{arm} is one generator's worth of answers over the whole grid. The \textbf{grid} is the fixed set of 32,000 answers; the four embedding systems in it are the \textbf{testbed}.

An answer is an \textbf{attempt} if the refusal rule does not match it, and a \textbf{refusal} otherwise. Results reported as \emph{answered-only} exclude refusals. \textbf{Paired} means the comparison is restricted to queries every system answered, so the systems are compared on the same questions.

The \textbf{spread} in a cell is the difference in mean faithfulness between the best and worst of the four systems. An evaluator's \textbf{resolving power} is how small a difference it can detect at a given sample size. The same spread may reach significance under one evaluator and not another purely because one evaluator's per-query scores are less noisy. That is a separate thing from how strongly it reacts to an error. The \textbf{floor} is what an evaluator scores for an untouched answer against an unrelated document, which is above zero and differs by evaluator, so a \textbf{floor-anchored} gate is one placed relative to it rather than at a fixed constant.


\section{How Sensitive Are These Evaluators to a Known Error?}
\label{sec:sensitivity}


\subsection{A controlled falsification}
\label{sec:falsification}

A faithfulness score is only useful if it responds when an answer stops being faithful, so we construct that condition directly. For each answer containing a numeric value that appears in the retrieved context, we replace the value with one that does not appear there, preserve the surface format, and re-score against the same chunks. Two eligibility rules carry the design: the original value must be present, or the answer was never grounded, and the replacement must be absent, or the altered answer is accidentally supported and a low score is correct behaviour.

Cases are taken deterministically, in dataset order rather than by random sampling: the first 200 eligible answers per system, dataset and generator. The scored set is therefore a prefix of an already filtered population: 6,179 cases against 9,286 eligible. HotpotQA/Mistral-7B is a full census, 595 of 595, because it has fewer than 200 eligible answers on every system. The rest run from a near-census on GPT-4o-mini (800 of 882 on NQ) to two fifths on Claude (800 of 1,946).

Two controls accompany it: an entity substitution, which makes a comparable edit to a named entity instead of a value, and the untouched answer scored against another query's context, which gives the floor of each evaluator's scale. Cases are rebuilt deterministically, so all three evaluators see identical inputs.

On Claude's Natural Questions answers, NLI-max leaves the falsified answer at 0.852 against a floor of 0.474. That clears the fixed 0.5 gate and does not clear the floor-anchored gate for that cell, which sits at the 95th percentile of the floor distribution, 0.985. The same edit on GPT-4o-mini's answers lands at 0.436, essentially at that generator's floor of 0.506. The two open-weight arms fall between: Qwen at 0.574 against a floor of 0.470, and Mistral-7B at 0.567 against 0.492. Evaluator, context and perturbation code are identical. Only the generator that wrote the answer differs.

One case shows the comparison at its plainest. On the same Natural Questions query, with the same chunks retrieved by all-mpnet-base-v2, Claude answers ``According to the provided context, season 2 of Jessica Jones is scheduled to be released on March 8, 2018.'' and GPT-4o-mini answers ``Season 2 of Jessica Jones is scheduled to be released on March 8, 2018.'' The probe changes March 8 to March 13 in both. NLI-max moves Claude's answer from 0.985 to 0.983 and GPT-4o-mini's from 0.905 to 0.068. AlignScore moves them from 0.971 to 0.054 and from 0.986 to 0.018. Both answers carry a single assertion, so claim-min equals NLI-max on each. They differ only in the opening clause, which Section~\ref{sec:assertions} returns to.

We predicted Qwen's intermediate position before that arm was run, from its assertion count alone (Section~\ref{sec:assertions}), and it holds in both datasets under all three evaluators. Mistral-7B, added later at a similar assertion count, lands in the same band: on Natural Questions the two agree to within a point under every evaluator, and on HotpotQA they differ by 2.3 to 7.2 detection points. The evaluators order consistently too, but the ordering has to name its quantity. AlignScore has the largest mean drop in all eight dataset-generator cells of the probe, and claim-min detects more falsifications than NLI-max in all eight. The full ordering --- AlignScore, then claim-min, then NLI-max --- holds in seven of the eight by detection rate and five of the eight by mean drop. Claim-min edges AlignScore on Natural Questions under Claude (25.1\% against 24.1\%), and NLI-max edges claim-min by mean drop on HotpotQA under three of the four generators. Generators differ in how far apart the evaluators are, not in which of them responds least.

What stability there is belongs to this grid rather than to the evaluators, and we did not probe outside it. The falsification probe ran on these two datasets only, so we cannot say whether the detection ordering survives a regime where retrieval fails. A different ordering does not survive it: on QASPER the evaluators' ranking by mean score inverts for one of the three generators run there (\movedref~\ref{sec:qasper}). An ordering that looks like a property of the evaluators can be a property of the regime.

The evaluators do not share a floor, so detection rates only become comparable once the scale is normalised, and we report two gates. On Claude's NQ cell an untouched answer scored against an unrelated context averages 0.474 under NLI-max and 0.245 under claim-min. The distribution is heavy-tailed: its 95th percentile is 0.985 under NLI-max, so an unrelated document clears a fixed 0.5 gate nearly half the time. Our second gate sits relative to each evaluator's own floor rather than at a shared constant, which is why we call it floor-anchored, and we place it at that percentile. There, "detected" means the falsified answer is no longer better supported than an unrelated document --- the same statement on every scale. Read that way NLI-max detects 50.2\% of falsifications rather than 5.1\%, and its gap to GPT-4o-mini narrows from roughly elevenfold to under twofold. At that gate the extremes mostly hold: Claude detects least and GPT-4o-mini most in five of the six dataset-evaluator rows. The exception is AlignScore on Natural Questions, where the floor-anchored gate compresses the four generators into 75.5\% to 82.1\% and Claude edges Qwen. The evaluator ordering does not carry over to that gate. There AlignScore detects least of the three on NQ/GPT-4o-mini, and claim-min falls below NLI-max on HotpotQA/Claude (Appendix~\ref{app:falsification}). The floor-anchored gate is a comparison device, not an operating point: it sits above the mean untouched answer.



Counterfactual substitution is also used to build training data. \citet{ju-etal-2026} construct 99,094 examples by replacing answer-bearing entities in SQuAD and TriviaQA with type-consistent alternatives, so that following the context can be distinguished from agreeing with it by coincidence. The construction is close to ours and the purpose is not: they manufacture conflicts to train a generator toward the context, and we manufacture them to ask what a scorer does when the conflict is already there. Their scale also shows what ours is not, since our probe is 200 cases per system, dataset and generator, chosen for having a gold-standard error rather than for covering the space of errors.

\subsection{Answers with more assertions are scored less sensitively}
\label{sec:assertions}

Segmenting each answer into sentence-level assertions and grouping by count gives a declining relationship in all four generators. It is not strictly monotone, and the exceptions all sit in thin tail buckets (Appendix~\ref{app:assertions}). Under NLI-max, Claude's response stops falling after four assertions, Qwen and Mistral-7B each rise once in the five-or-more bucket, and GPT-4o-mini rises at four assertions and falls again, on buckets of 10 and 7 answers.

On Natural Questions the four generators average 3.08, 1.77, 1.72 and 1.25 assertions per attempted answer, counted over all their attempted answers and not only the falsified ones, with median lengths of 55, 25, 20 and 15 words. That accounts for most of the cross-generator difference in Table~\ref{tab:2}. A falsified value is a smaller and smaller edit to the hypothesis as correct surrounding text accumulates, and the remainder dominates the entailment judgement. It does not account for all of it: within the single-assertion bucket the generators still differ, +0.102 against +0.385, +0.409 and +0.485. As a check on the claim-level implementation, every single-assertion answer scores identically under both aggregates (n = 3,249, largest absolute difference 2.3e-05, which is batch-padding noise). That check is what surfaced the normalisation defect in \movedref~\ref{sec:normalisation}, where it failed on all 131 formatted answers by as much as 0.729.

Claim-level aggregation is a partial remedy. As a fraction of the distance to the random-context floor, the median response on Claude's answers moves from 0.01 to 0.03, and the minimum moves the right way in 55\% of cases; when the falsified assertion is not already the weakest, the minimum does not move at all. A second dilution survives inside assertions, since the four generators still differ at matched assertion count and their single-assertion answers average 23.9, 19.5, 17.2 and 16.1 words.

The open-weight generators make that residual legible. Qwen sits between Claude and GPT-4o-mini in aggregate and does not once assertion count is held fixed: at one assertion it is +0.409, against +0.485 for GPT-4o-mini and +0.102 for Claude. Its intermediate aggregate comes from its assertion \emph{distribution}, not from intermediate per-assertion sensitivity. Mistral-7B replicates that. It averages 1.72 assertions against Qwen's 1.77 and responds by +0.385 at a single assertion, an interval that overlaps Qwen's, so two separately trained models agree both on how much they write and on what a falsified value costs them. Claude remains the outlier at +0.102. So the remaining gap is not a smooth function of verbosity. Two generators cannot tell those apart; the third makes it visible and the fourth replicates it.

Part of what sets Claude apart is how it opens an answer. It begins 97.5\% of its attempted answers with an attribution clause such as ``Based on the provided context,'' or ``According to the provided context,''. Qwen opens 12.4\% of its attempts that way, Mistral-7B 17.6\% and GPT-4o-mini 0.2\%, pooled over both datasets and all four systems. Splitting the single-assertion Natural Questions answers on that clause, Qwen responds by +0.457 [+0.418, +0.495] without it and +0.129 [+0.076, +0.187] with it, and Mistral-7B by +0.426 [+0.387, +0.467] and +0.162 [+0.091, +0.238] (bootstrap 95\% intervals). Claude's 91 single-assertion answers all carry it, and its +0.102 [+0.064, +0.146] sits inside both of those preamble intervals. That split is observational, so we tested it directly. On the probe's own cases, rebuilt exactly (the as-written scores reproduce the published ones to the last digit), we removed the clause from both the original and the falsified answer and scored them again. On single-assertion answers the response rises. Claude's goes from +0.102 to +0.272 on Natural Questions (paired difference +0.169, 95\% interval [+0.098, +0.237]) and from +0.149 to +0.470 on HotpotQA, and NLI-max detection rises from 10.1\% to 42.5\% and from 20.4\% to 80.6\%. Mistral-7B's single-assertion answers move the same way on Natural Questions, from +0.162 to +0.291. On HotpotQA that closes the gap to the other generators' answers without the clause (+0.401 to +0.426). On Natural Questions it closes a little under half of it, and the rest is not explained. The clause also lifts the score of the untouched answer: Claude's single-assertion originals score 0.950 with it and 0.788 without it on Natural Questions. So it raises the score of a grounded answer and shields a falsified one at once. At two or more assertions removing it changes little (+0.038 for Claude on Natural Questions, and nothing distinguishable from zero in any other cell), so there the dilution described above carries the effect. The other two evaluators confirm the effect, so it is not a quirk of the entailment model. Claim-min equals NLI-max on single-assertion answers by construction. At two or more assertions removing the clause raises Claude's claim-min detection from 28.0\% to 39.1\% on Natural Questions and from 42.5\% to 62.4\% on HotpotQA, though its mean response moves by only +0.032 and +0.041. AlignScore is shielded less but measurably. Claude's single-assertion response under it rises from +0.497 to +0.656 on Natural Questions (paired difference [+0.120, +0.196]) and from +0.678 to +0.782 on HotpotQA, and by about +0.025 at two or more assertions. The clause is not stripped for any evaluator, so the treatment is identical across them. It is the same kind of choice as the markdown normalisation of \movedref~\ref{sec:normalisation}, and we report it rather than re-run the grid without it.



\subsection{Verbosity, varied within one model}
\label{sec:verbosity}

The gradient in Section~\ref{sec:assertions} runs across four generators, and those generators differ in more than answer length. Two are proprietary chat models from different labs and two are open-weight models from different labs; assertion count is confounded with everything else that distinguishes them. Each further model adds a further confounded point. Varying the prompt on one model does not.

We ran Qwen2.5-7B a second time over the same queries, the same retrieved contexts and the same weights, adding one line to the prompt asking for a fuller answer. The grounding instruction and the refusal sentence are byte-identical to the standard template, so the refusal rule still matches and faithfulness is still measured against the same constraint. The arm carries its own label throughout.

The manipulation is large. Assertions per attempted answer go from 1.77 to 4.90 on Natural Questions and 1.54 to 4.99 on HotpotQA; attempted answers go from 34 and 26 words to 108 and 110. That overshoots the most verbose generator in the main grid, which averages 3.08, so the arm extends the axis rather than interpolating on it.

Sensitivity falls in all six cells (Table~\ref{tab:verbosity}), and no confidence interval overlaps its own baseline. Under the entailment aggregate detection goes from 35.1\% to 10.4\% on Natural Questions and 54.8\% to 17.4\% on HotpotQA. The effect is not specific to the evaluator: AlignScore falls furthest in proportional terms, from 74.5\% to 18.5\% on HotpotQA, and its mean response to a falsified value drops from 0.494 to 0.127. The instrument Section~\ref{sec:falsification} measures as most responsive is not exempt; it had been reading shorter answers.

Claim-min degrades least, retaining 28.9\% and 43.6\% where the others fall to between 10\% and 19\%. That is the behaviour its construction predicts and it is here demonstrated by manipulation rather than inferred across models. Its absolute scores, though, collapse: mean 0.693 to 0.234 on Natural Questions and 0.424 to 0.076 on HotpotQA. A minimum over five assertions sits near zero whatever the answer says, so claim-min keeps its sensitivity at this verbosity while ceasing to be usable against a fixed threshold. Resisting the structural bias and remaining thresholdable are separate properties.

\textbf{What this does not show.} We predicted, before running it, that an arm at 4.9 assertions would fall below the 5.1\% detection of the main grid's most verbose generator at 3.08. It does not: 10.4\% against 5.1\%, on more assertions. Within one model, then, changing answer structure moves detection, and on this evidence it accounts for most of the distance between the two generators but not all of it. What remains is not explained by verbosity, and Section~\ref{sec:assertions} traces part of it to a leading attribution clause. The manipulation is not perfectly clean, since the same prompt line also moves refusals and the scale floor (below). Section~\ref{sec:assertions} reaches the same conclusion from the opposite direction, by conditioning on assertion count and finding one generator still separated from the other three. On HotpotQA the residual is small enough to disappear: 0.088 here against 0.085 there.

The verbose prompt also raised the share of the probe's cases opening with that clause, from 24.2\% to 47.2\% on Natural Questions and from 8.2\% to 27.6\% on HotpotQA, so the clause could have carried the effect. It does not. Among answers without it, NLI-max detection falls from 41.0\% to 10.4\% and from 57.8\% to 17.1\%.

\textbf{Two effects that are not the one we manipulated.} Refusals fall, from 11.8\% to 9.8\% and 29.6\% to 23.8\%, so the answered population is not identical between arms; the probe runs on attempted answers, but the two arms attempt slightly different questions. And the scale floor rises steeply. An untouched answer scored against an unrelated document averages 0.470 under the entailment aggregate on Natural Questions for the standard arm and 0.581 for the verbose one; on HotpotQA it goes from 0.245 to \textbf{0.509}. A five-assertion answer gives an unrelated context more chances to support something, and the aggregate takes the maximum. On HotpotQA that puts the unrelated-document baseline above the fixed 0.5 gate entirely, which is the argument of Section~\ref{sec:falsification} for a floor-anchored gate, arrived at here by manipulation: verbosity degrades a fixed threshold twice over, by moving the falsified answer up and the floor up with it.

\section{The Verdict Depends on the Evaluator's Resolving Power}
\label{sec:verdict}

We compare the four retrieval systems on faithfulness, refusals excluded, paired on a common query subset, with a paired bootstrap of 10,000 resamples.

The result of this section is narrow, and we state it before the table. Which evaluator is used decides whether a comparison of these systems returns a difference or a null. It does not follow that the evaluators rank the systems differently, and a direct test finds no evidence that they do (Appendix~\ref{app:omnibus}). What separates them is resolving power, and it has two parts. AlignScore's paired per-query differences are less noisy, so it has the smallest minimum detectable effect in every cell (Appendix~\ref{app:power}). After that variance is divided out, its standardised effect is also larger in five of eight cells (Appendix~\ref{app:standardised}), but not on Mistral-7B, where the entailment aggregate has the larger response in both datasets. Both parts lead to the same consequence for a published comparison.

The statistic is the spread, the difference in mean faithfulness between the best and worst of the four systems, with a p-value from a paired bootstrap over that pair re-centred at zero \citep{dror-etal-2018}. We report it as a deliberately extreme sensitivity check, and not as the loss a practitioner should expect. Someone who adopts one of these systems stands to lose the distance from it to the best of the four. That equals the spread only when the system they adopted is the worst one, and is smaller under the other three choices. The spread is the largest shortfall the choice can produce, which is the right quantity for asking whether the choice can matter at all and the wrong one for asking what it costs on average. It also summarises two systems picked for being the extremes, so its p-value is not adjusted for that selection and the two intermediate systems never enter it. Appendix~\ref{app:omnibus} runs two tests that do pay for the selection. The permutation test retains two of the four comparisons, in two dataset-generator cells, and the rank-based omnibus retains one. The direction across all four systems is in \movedref~\ref{sec:direction}.

Under the common evaluator one of the eight cells shows a detectable difference, on Mistral-7B; under the evaluator Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} measures as most responsive to known errors, two do. All four surviving comparisons are on HotpotQA, which has the wider retrieval spread, and all four Natural Questions cells are null. That may be a property of how the Natural Questions sample is drawn rather than of the evaluators. It retains only queries whose annotated answer falls inside the retained context window, which leaves a constrained single-hop task with less for an embedding model to get wrong. We cannot separate that from the two datasets differing in hop structure. Appendix~\ref{app:power} reports what each cell could have detected: the minimum detectable effect at 80\% power is 0.012 to 0.064, above the observed spread in twenty of the twenty-four dataset-generator-evaluator cells, and it is lowest under AlignScore in all eight. Part of the difference between evaluators here is resolving power at fixed $n$, not only response to a known error. Appendix~\ref{app:standardised} separates the two: dividing each spread by its own paired standard deviation removes the variance term, and AlignScore's standardised effect still exceeds the entailment aggregate's in five of eight cells. So lower variance is not the whole of it: in five cells AlignScore also responds more strongly relative to its noise. On Mistral-7B the ordering reverses and the entailment aggregate is the more responsive of the two in both datasets, consistent with that arm breaking on HotpotQA under NLI-max and claim-min and not under AlignScore. A result reported only under the first would describe the resolution of the instrument, not a property of the systems. None of the four is on Claude, whose answers the entailment aggregate resolves least well.

Each added generator made the correction stricter --- twelve comparisons, then eighteen, now twenty-four --- and the count rose each time. The design does not guarantee that. Adding generators also qualified what had been the sharpest result here: under AlignScore on HotpotQA, three of the four generators rank the four systems identically (all-mpnet $<$ text-embedding-3-small $<$ BGE-M3 $<$ E5-instruct), where under NLI-max no two of those three agree. Mistral-7B is the exception, ranking them text-embedding-3-small $<$ all-mpnet $<$ E5-instruct $<$ BGE-M3, and it is also the one generator whose AlignScore spread is not distinguishable from zero (Table~\ref{tab:7}), so that ordering is read off differences inside its own noise. Read the agreement carefully in any case. The generators are independent of each other but answer from the \emph{same} retrieved contexts, so this is three generators agreeing about a property of the shared retrieval, not three replications. What it establishes is that one evaluator resolves that property consistently across most generators and another does not, on identical inputs.

The count of four is not invariant to the correction either. Three comparisons sit either side of alpha = 0.05 depending on which are treated as one family. NQ/Claude under AlignScore is p = 0.0081 uncorrected, adjusted to 0.154 over the twenty-four in the table, 0.049 over the eight sharing an evaluator, 0.024 over the three in its own cell. HotpotQA/GPT-4o-mini under claim-min is p = 0.0070, adjusted to 0.140, 0.049 and 0.014. HotpotQA/Mistral-7B under AlignScore is p = 0.0185, adjusted to 0.333, 0.093 and 0.019. Correcting within evaluator makes the count six and correcting within cell makes it seven. Correcting within evaluator is defensible, since the eight NLI tests establish which cells are null and so precede the question rather than competing with it. We still report the twenty-four-comparison family, because it is the most conservative and was fixed before the correction was applied, and choosing a smaller one after seeing that it restores cells is a selection we could not account for. We give the alternative as well as the choice, because a count that moves with the multiplicity family depends on an analysis decision in the same way this paper reports for the choice of evaluator.

The statistic moves it too, and in the less comfortable direction. Appendix~\ref{app:omnibus} replaces the paired test on the extreme pair with a permutation test that re-selects the extremes in every resample, so the cost of choosing them is paid: two comparisons survive Holm rather than four, HotpotQA/GPT-4o-mini under AlignScore and HotpotQA/Mistral-7B under NLI-max. A rank-based omnibus over the same four systems leaves one comparison, the second of those. The two counts answer different questions. A practitioner comparing their adopted model against the best alternative is exposed to the extreme pair, while the omnibus asks whether the four differ at all. The paired figure is the most permissive of the three, so we report all of them. The same appendix tests whether the evaluators disagree about the four systems, rather than merely returning different verdicts about them, and finds no detectable interaction in any of the eight cells. Read with Appendix~\ref{app:standardised}, the defensible claim is that these evaluators resolve a common pattern with different resolving power. That leaves the consequence for a published comparison unchanged and makes the mechanism narrower than evaluator disagreement.

\section{What an Equivalence Claim Requires}
\label{sec:equivalence}

Five of the eight cells in Table~\ref{tab:7} show no detectable difference under any evaluator. Appendix~\ref{app:power} puts the minimum detectable effect above the observed spread in twenty of the twenty-four dataset-generator-evaluator cells, so an effect the size of the one we measured would usually not have been detected. A null here is not evidence of equivalence, so we test for equivalence directly with two one-sided tests \citep{schuirmann-1987}. TOST turns on the margin, and the margin is a judgement about what difference would matter. We sweep it from $\pm$0.01 to $\pm$0.10 and report the curve (Appendix~\ref{app:tost}).

The curve is steep. On NQ/Claude under NLI-max no pair is equivalent at $\pm$0.01, four of six are at $\pm$0.02 and all six at $\pm$0.03. HotpotQA needs a far wider margin. No pair there is equivalent at $\pm$0.01 under any evaluator, and below $\pm$0.05 only four of its eighteen generator-evaluator rows contain any equivalent pair at all: AlignScore on Claude, which reaches five of six at $\pm$0.03, and AlignScore on Mistral-7B and both NLI-based aggregates on GPT-4o-mini, with one each. At the other end, HotpotQA under Mistral-7B is the only cell where $\pm$0.10 still leaves a pair non-equivalent, under NLI-max and under claim-min. Stating equivalence means choosing a column, and the choice does most of the work.

Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} gives a way to choose it. Falsifying one grounded value moves NLI-max by 0.049 to 0.095 on Claude's answers, so $\pm$0.05 on that evaluator is about the size of one fabricated fact, and equivalence at that margin says the systems differ by less than one fabrication. The anchor is evaluator-specific and so is the verdict: the same falsification moves claim-min by 0.093 to 0.179 and AlignScore by 0.243 to 0.364 on those answers, and at $\pm$0.03 on NQ/Claude, NLI-max calls all six pairs equivalent while claim-min calls two. An equivalence claim has to name its evaluator, and say what the margin is worth on that evaluator's scale.

The anchor fixes the units, not the tolerance. Saying $\pm$0.05 is about one fabricated fact on this evaluator does not establish that one fabricated fact is the right bar, and nothing in our data can establish that. A system summarising product reviews and a system reading a drug label have different tolerances for the same measured difference, and the second may want a margin an order of magnitude tighter. What the anchor removes is the case where a margin is chosen because it is conventional and its magnitude on the chosen evaluator's scale is never checked. Reporting the curve leaves the choice with the reader, who knows the application.

One limit on the anchor runs in a direction we can name. It is calibrated on errors we constructed, and \citet{sinha-2025} report that naturally occurring hallucinations are harder to separate than constructed ones, because a real hallucination stays semantically closer to a faithful answer (Limitations). If that holds for these evaluators, one real fabrication moves the score by less than the 0.049 to 0.095 we measure. A margin set at one constructed fabrication is then worth more than one real fabrication, and equivalence declared at it tolerates more than the anchor appears to allow. We cannot size that error, but its sign is fixed: calibrating against constructed errors makes a margin permissive, never strict. Anyone holding labelled natural errors from their own traffic should re-anchor the margin on those instead.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

For work reporting RAG faithfulness:

\begin{itemize}
\item Name the evaluator. "Faithfulness did not differ" is incomplete: in three of our eight cells the same data returns a difference under one evaluator and a null under another. That is a statement about which instrument was used. The evaluators are not shown to rank the systems differently, and the instrument is what the reader needs in order to know what the null is worth.
\item Report the evaluation population, and whether membership depends on the system being compared. Pooling refusals here produced a column tracking retrieval quality in all four generators, in opposite directions, and the direction is predictable from whether that generator's refusals outscore its attempts (Table~\ref{tab:4}). Where a framework already scores refusals separately, this is a caution about aggregate reporting rather than a defect in the framework. It reached our own pipeline during development, where two implementations of the shared refusal rule disagreed on 365 rows. That is the more realistic failure: the rule exists, and two parts of the pipeline apply different versions of it. We found it before computing any number reported here, and every reported number comes from the single implementation that replaced both.
\item When individual answers are being checked for a fabricated value, prefer claim-level aggregation to a maximum over the whole answer, most of all where answers carry several assertions. For comparing systems the relevant property is a different one (\movedref~\ref{sec:choosing}). Treat it as a partial remedy. It lifts detection on the verbose generator's NQ cell almost fivefold, from 5.1\% to 25.1\%, and 25.1\% is still a minority of known fabrications, against 61.0\% for the tersest generator under the same aggregate. The gap between generators narrows and does not close, so claim-min reduces the structural bias rather than removing it. It also costs something the maximum does not. Its absolute scores collapse at that verbosity, from a mean of 0.693 to 0.234 on Natural Questions and 0.424 to 0.076 on HotpotQA, because a minimum over five assertions sits near zero whatever the answer says. Resisting the structural bias and remaining thresholdable are separate properties, and claim-level aggregation buys the first at the cost of the second: a fixed grounding gate carried across to it unchanged would reject almost every verbose answer. Per-assertion thresholding, or weighting assertions by how much of the answer's content they carry, are the obvious next steps and we did not test them.
\item For symbolic content, put a deterministic check in front of the evaluator. Requiring every value in an answer to appear in the retrieved context flags every falsification we constructed. That part is circular and we state it as such: our second eligibility rule defines the substituted value as absent from the retrieved context, so a containment test must catch it. The informative comparison is the other direction. A trained evaluator tested on the same cases reaches 89.4\% at best and 5.1\% at worst, so these edits are not hard to catch --- they are hard for an entailment aggregate to catch, and the check is indifferent to answer length where the aggregates are not. What the probe does measure about the check is its false-positive rate, 0.8\% to 5.2\% on untouched answers, and that rate is not circular.
\item Calibrate the margin against a measured effect before claiming equivalence. A falsification experiment of this kind costs nothing beyond re-scoring. Constructed errors are the easy case, so a margin anchored on them is permissive rather than strict (Section~\ref{sec:equivalence}).
\end{itemize}

For a document question answering system, the practical reading of Table~\ref{tab:2} is that a grounding threshold on a verbose generator catches a minority of falsified values: 5.1\% under the common evaluator, and 25.1\% under claim-min, the best of the three on that cell. That is the case for the deterministic check above, and two qualifications on it matter. Numbers that assert nothing about the world, such as chunk indices and ordered-list markers, are not expected in the retrieved text, and counting them raises that rate on Claude's HotpotQA answers to about 39\%. The rest are quantities the answer derives instead of copying, which a literal check also flags, so it complements an entailment score rather than replacing one.

Checking symbolic content against the source is an old idea \citep{goodrich-etal-2019, nan-etal-2021, zhao-etal-2020}, and that numbers are a weak point is documented at the level of the representations too \citep{wallace-etal-2019}. We claim no novelty for it. We do claim the measurement that makes the case for it here, against three current evaluators on identical inputs, and note that a practitioner choosing an instrument needs to know which one works. Entailment is separately a loose proxy for attribution: the AIS framework \citep{rashkin-etal-2023} separates support by an identified source from general plausibility.

What this paper adds is narrower than the check: the controlled minimal pair. One numeric value is replaced in an otherwise byte-identical generation, and the same case goes to all three evaluators. What is measured is each evaluator's response to a known error on identical input, not its agreement with a human label on some other corpus. That covers numeric values, four generators and two datasets.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Holding a 32,000-answer generation grid fixed and varying only the faithfulness evaluator changes the conclusion about whether embedding choice affects faithfulness in four of twenty-four comparisons, which fall in three of eight dataset-generator cells, or in two comparisons in two cells under a test that pays for selecting the pair compared. The evaluators are not shown to disagree about the systems; they resolve the same differences with different resolving power, and that is sufficient to move a published verdict. The differences between evaluators are directly measurable. A falsified grounded value moves the common entailment aggregate by 0.054 on one generator, 0.251 and 0.261 on two more and 0.451 on a fourth, and how many assertions an answer contains accounts for most of that gap. A faithfulness result that does not name its evaluator, population, aggregation and equivalence margin is not reproducible in the sense that matters: another group making different standard choices on the same generations would reach a different conclusion.

\section*{Code and Data Availability}

The pipeline, the three evaluator interfaces, the falsification probe, the refusal rule and every analysis script behind the tables in this paper are available at \url{https://github.com/Shakhriyorbek/rag-faithfulness}. The repository includes a map from each method claim in this paper to the function that implements it, and scripts that regenerate each table from stored checkpoints. This matters for a paper whose central recommendation is to re-run the probe on one's own outputs: the probe is roughly 200 cases per cell and needs no API access once generations exist. The generated answers and their scores are retained and can be shared on request; we do not redistribute the source datasets, which are publicly available.


\section*{Limitations}

What we can claim is bounded by the grid we measured on, and by the graders that decide who belongs to each population.

\begin{itemize}
\item Four embedding models, two English datasets, four generators. Two things this bounds should be separated, because our controls move one and not the other. The \emph{axis} the mechanism in Section~\ref{sec:assertions} runs along is assertions per answer. As prompted, the four generators span 1.25 to 3.08 assertions per attempted answer on Natural Questions and 1.08 to 2.39 on HotpotQA. The prompt-varied arm of Section~\ref{sec:verbosity} carries one of them to 4.90 and 4.99 on the same two datasets, where the effect continues in the direction the mechanism predicts. The span of the axis we have measured is therefore about twice the generators' own span on Natural Questions and three times on HotpotQA. The \emph{population} of models is four, and no control here changes that. Varying a prompt holds the weights fixed. That is what makes it a clean test of the confound between length and model identity, and equally what stops it saying anything about models trained differently, so the prompt-varied arm does not raise the population to five. All four are instruction-tuned chat models from broadly similar recipes, and two of them are 7B open-weight models. Part of the residual that answer structure fails to explain is an attribution clause that one generator uses on almost every answer. Removing it closes the single-assertion gap on HotpotQA and a little under half of it on Natural Questions, where the remainder is unexplained. Whether any of this holds for a much larger model, a much smaller one, or a different training regime is untested.
\item Correctness is graded by the judge over the thirty-two cells of the matched grid and nowhere else. Within that grid it covers all four generators, so \movedref~\ref{sec:refusals} and every figure conditioned on correctness are four-generator results. Outside it the judge was not run. The jina control (\movedref~\ref{sec:control}), QASPER (\movedref~\ref{sec:qasper}) and the floor, ceiling and arrangement conditions (\movedref~\ref{sec:anchors}) are graded by string containment instead. The item below shows that containment is biased in both directions, by a margin that varies with the generator and the dataset. The accuracy figures reported for those arms are therefore not directly comparable with the matched grid's, and we do not pool them. Nothing in Sections~\ref{sec:sensitivity}, \ref{sec:verdict} or \ref{sec:equivalence} depends on that grading.

\item The grader we use in place of string overlap is biased, and we chose it knowing the bias. Exact match is 0.000 in every cell we measured, because the generators do not emit bare answer spans. String containment is the obvious fallback, and it bounds accuracy in neither direction. Measured against the judge it understates accuracy by 5 to 16 points in twenty of the twenty-four cells where both signals exist. It overstates accuracy by 2 to 6 points in the four HotpotQA cells under Claude, where a long answer mentions a reference string while asserting something else. The judge is itself a language model reading model output, and we have not checked it against a human annotator on this data.
\item Retrieval hit is defined as the gold answer string appearing in the retrieved context. This is a strong definition, and it does not separate retrieval failure from failures of composition, entity resolution or inference once the evidence is present.
\item The Natural Questions sample retains only queries whose annotated answer falls inside the retained context window. This makes the retrieval task more constrained than open-domain RAG, where evidence may be distributed, partial or implicit. It may also be why all four Natural Questions cells are null under every evaluator while all three cells with a surviving comparison are on HotpotQA. A constrained single-hop task leaves less for an embedding model to get wrong, so the systems have less to differ about. We cannot separate that from the datasets differing in hop structure, and do not claim to.
\item Pairing on the queries every system answered removes exactly those queries where retrieval quality determined whether an answer was produced, so the comparison is conditional on a subset selected partly by the systems being compared. Substituting each system's own answered rows moves the spread by at most 0.022 and reorders the systems in eleven of the twenty-four combinations, three of eight under NLI-max. It leaves the ordering unchanged in three of the four comparisons that survive correction. The exception is HotpotQA/Mistral-7B under claim-min, whose ordering does change, so that comparison is in part conditional on the pairing and we do not read an ordering off it. The pairing cannot explain disagreement between evaluators in any case, because it is identical across them.
\item Where we do detect a difference it is small: 0.041 to 0.080 in mean faithfulness across the four surviving comparisons, all on HotpotQA, whose systems span 11.9 NDCG@5 points. They are detectable, but they are not large.
\item The three evaluators receive byte-identical answers but not identical premises. The entailment scorer is given each of the five retrieved chunks separately, plus their concatenation truncated to the model's window, and the maximum is taken. The claim-level scorer applies that construction per extracted claim. AlignScore is given the concatenated context as one premise and performs its own splitting. Each scorer is used as its authors intend, but that means some of the difference reported in Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} comes from premise granularity and not from evaluator sensitivity. We bound that share by re-scoring the same cases with the same entailment model on the concatenated premise alone. We ran that control in all eight dataset-generator cells, and the answer depends on which quantity is asked about, so we give both. As a fraction of the NLI-max-to-AlignScore gap in mean drop it runs from -0.55 to +0.20, negative in four cells: by that measure a concatenated premise makes the entailment model \emph{less} responsive. As a fraction of the gap in detection at the 0.5 gate it runs from +0.01 to +0.62 and is positive in all eight, because the concatenated premise also lowers the untouched answer's score and so moves more answers across the gate. What holds under both is the comparison that matters: AlignScore exceeds the same entailment model given AlignScore's own premise in every one of the eight cells, by mean drop and by detection. Premise granularity accounts for at most about three fifths of the detection gap and none of the mean-drop gap in half the cells, so it does not account for AlignScore's advantage. The hypothesis is normalised identically for all three scorers (\movedref~\ref{sec:normalisation}). Before that normalisation was applied during development, the difference alone decided one cell.
\item Which answers count as refusals is fixed by a rule rather than given by the data, and that rule sets the evaluation population for every result from Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} onward. It is a researcher degree of freedom as much as a finding. Swapping one defensible detector for another moves the rows nearest the boundary between refusing and attempting, and carries the uncorrected p-value across the conventional threshold in two of the twenty-four comparisons (\movedref~\ref{sec:graders}). Neither of those two survives correction for multiple comparisons, but the population every reported number rests on still depends on the choice. The rule used here matches a wider list of declining openings than the prompt's own refusal sentence and strips a leading preamble first; without those two extensions 759 refusals would be graded as attempts, 602 of them Mistral-7B's. Appendix~\ref{app:refusalrule} reports the headline count under each of the three defensible rules available to us. The rule used here, the same rule without its two extensions, and the judge's classification all select the same four comparisons, so the uncorrected p-values move with the rule while the corrected count does not.
\item The falsification cases are the first 200 eligible answers in dataset order for each system, dataset and generator, rather than a random sample of the eligible answers: 6,179 cases scored against 9,286 eligible across the grid. Eligibility requires a grounded numeric value in the answer together with a replacement value absent from the context, so the scored set is a prefix of an already filtered population. Cells whose eligible count is near 200 are close to a census, and HotpotQA/Mistral-7B is one; the largest cells are sampled at about two fifths. Appendix~\ref{app:prefix} tests whether that prefix is exchangeable with the eligible set by regressing detection on position within it; no cell shows a trend that survives correction over the eight tests.
\item The repository configures seven embedding models. Four carry the matched comparison --- all-mpnet-base-v2 (contrastive), BGE-M3 (multilingual), E5-large-instruct (instruction-tuned) and text-embedding-3-small (proprietary) --- chosen to span pretraining paradigms and to include one closed-source arm. A fifth, jina-embeddings-v3 \citep{sturua-etal-2024}, was run over both datasets and all four generators and is reported in \movedref~\ref{sec:control} as a control on the matched set, not a member of it, because it fails the matched-quality precondition on Natural Questions. GTE-large and Instructor-XL remain unrun: each additional model is a further generation pass over every dataset and generator, and the grid is a testbed for the evaluator comparison rather than a survey of embedding models.

\item The floor, ceiling and context-arrangement conditions of \movedref~\ref{sec:anchors} were run on the Claude arm only. The geometric analysis of \movedref~\ref{sec:esa} covers the four matched systems and uses no generator, and re-ranking was run on one embedding system with Claude. Neither the anchor conditions nor re-ranking has been shown to hold across generators, and the re-ranking result in particular is a single operating point ($\lambda = 0.6$) on a single system, so it bounds what that intervention achieved here. It says nothing about what such interventions can achieve in general.

\item The oracle condition supplies answer-bearing chunks only. That is narrower context than top-five retrieval, so it ceilings evidence selection and not accuracy, and retrieval exceeds it on Natural Questions. Defining the ceiling as gold passages in full would raise it, at the cost of comparability with what retrieval actually produces.

\item QASPER is reported as a boundary condition rather than a third dataset. Retrieval fails on it under every system tested, so the population it yields cannot test a claim about systems at matched retrieval quality, and the inverted evaluator ordering we observe there is measured on answers produced largely without supporting evidence.

\item All three datasets are English, and the two that carry the main grid are built on Wikipedia. The no-retrieval condition shows 32.2\% of Natural Questions is answerable from parametric memory alone, so the Wikipedia dependence is measured rather than hypothetical.
\item The detection rates here bear on deployment. A system that gates answers on a fixed grounding threshold, using the common entailment aggregate on a verbose generator, would pass 94.9\% of the answers in which we falsified a grounded value on Natural Questions. Anyone operating such a gate in a setting where a wrong number matters -- an invoice, a dosage, a contract term -- should measure its detection rate on their own generator's output before relying on it, because the rate is a property of that pairing and not of the threshold. We did not test deployment-scale traffic, and our probe injects errors rather than observing natural ones. That distinction may matter a great deal: \citet{sinha-2025} report embedding-based detection separating constructed hallucinations at a 0\% false-positive rate and naturally occurring ones at 100\%, on the grounds that real hallucinations stay semantically closer to a faithful answer than constructed ones do. If that holds for the evaluators we test, every detection rate in this paper is an upper bound on what the same gate would catch in deployment, and the verbose-generator figures are already low. The result is from an unrefereed preprint and we have not replicated it; we report it because it bounds our own method in the direction that matters. It bounds the equivalence margin in that same direction, since the margin is anchored on the same constructed errors (Section~\ref{sec:equivalence}).

\item The correct-versus-incorrect faithfulness comparison rests on 44 to 134 attempted wrong answers per cell. Excluding refusals and grading correctness with a judge both reduce that population, and the resulting confidence intervals are $\pm$0.064 to $\pm$0.129, that is 0.129 to 0.259 wide. The tests reported in \movedref~\ref{sec:refusals} bear on the general claim and do not resolve individual systems.
\end{itemize}

\section{What the comparison could have detected}
\label{app:power}

Five of the eight cells in Table~\ref{tab:7} are null under every evaluator, and \movedref~\ref{sec:control} shows the procedure separates systems that genuinely differ. Neither of those says what size of effect it would have caught. Table~\ref{tab:power} gives the minimum detectable effect at 80\% power for a two-sided paired test at $\alpha = 0.05$, computed from the observed standard deviation of the paired per-query differences between the best and worst system in each cell.

Two things follow, and the first is a caution about our own null results. The minimum detectable effect runs from 0.012 to 0.064 with a median of 0.036, and in twenty of the twenty-four dataset-generator-evaluator cells it exceeds the spread actually observed. Where we report no detectable difference, an effect the size of the one we measured would usually not have been detected. The nulls bound what a practitioner would notice at this sample size; they are not evidence that the systems are identical, which is why Section~\ref{sec:equivalence} tests equivalence separately instead of reading the nulls as equivalence. All four comparisons that do reach significance have a spread above their own minimum detectable effect (0.0461 against 0.0368, 0.0406 against 0.0350, 0.0796 against 0.0614 and 0.0699 against 0.0638), and no other comparison does.

The second is about the evaluators, and it qualifies how Section~\ref{sec:verdict} should be read. AlignScore has the lowest minimum detectable effect in all eight cells, 0.012 to 0.037, against 0.021 to 0.061 for NLI-max and 0.024 to 0.064 for claim-min. Some of that is a variance property: the standard deviation of the paired per-query difference is 0.12 to 0.30 under AlignScore and 0.20 to 0.48 under NLI-max, on identical answers. So part of why the verdict changes with the evaluator is that the evaluators differ in resolving power at fixed $n$, and not only in how they respond to a known error. Appendix~\ref{app:standardised} separates the two by dividing each spread by its own paired standard deviation. AlignScore's standardised effect remains the larger in five of eight cells, so the variance difference does not account for all of it, although it reverses on Mistral-7B.

\section{An omnibus test, and the system by evaluator interaction}
\label{app:omnibus}

The spread in Table~\ref{tab:7} is a maximum minus a minimum over four systems, so the two systems entering it are chosen for being extreme and its p-value is not adjusted for that choice. Two further tests remove that objection, and they qualify the headline.

\textbf{An omnibus that re-selects the extremes.} Permuting system labels independently within each query and recomputing the range makes the selection part of the null, since the extremes are chosen afresh in every resample. Over 10,000 resamples, and Holm-corrected across the twenty-four comparisons, \textbf{two comparisons survive} rather than four: HotpotQA/GPT-4o-mini under AlignScore at permutation $p = 0.0015$, and HotpotQA/Mistral-7B under NLI-max at $p = 0.0019$. HotpotQA/Mistral-7B under claim-min ($p = 0.0070$) and HotpotQA/Qwen under AlignScore ($p = 0.0076$) do not survive. A Friedman test over the same four systems asks the same question by ranks, and leaves \textbf{one} significant after the same correction: HotpotQA/Mistral-7B under NLI-max at $p = 0.0005$. Its next smallest uncorrected values are 0.0032 on that same cell under claim-min and 0.0092 on a cell the paired test does not pick out. Both omnibus tests are stricter than the spread test and both now retain a cell, where at eighteen comparisons the permutation test retained one and Friedman none.

So the count is four under a paired test on the extreme pair, two under a permutation test that pays for selecting that pair, and one under a rank-based omnibus. The paired figure is the test a practitioner comparing their adopted model against the best alternative would run. It is also the most permissive of the three, so a reader should see all of them.

\textbf{Do the evaluators disagree about the systems?} A verdict changing is not the same as two instruments ranking four systems differently. We test the second directly. Scores are standardised within evaluator, since the three share neither scale nor variance, and fitted as \texttt{z $\sim$ system * evaluator} with a random intercept per query, by maximum likelihood so that the fixed-effects likelihood ratio is valid.

\section{Is the probe's prefix exchangeable?}
\label{app:prefix}

Cases are the first 200 eligible answers in dataset order rather than a random 200, so the Wilson intervals beside each detection rate assume a sampling scheme we did not use. If detection does not vary with position inside the prefix, the prefix behaves like a random draw from the eligible set on the quantity being reported.

Detection by quartile of position under the entailment aggregate, pooled over the four embedding systems: on Natural Questions 3.2, 3.1, 7.9 and 6.7\% for Claude, 40.4, 33.1, 30.7 and 36.2\% for Qwen, 63.5, 55.4, 44.0 and 63.5\% for GPT-4o-mini and 39.6, 31.9, 34.9 and 31.2\% for Mistral-7B; on HotpotQA 12.8, 13.7, 18.4 and 10.9\%, then 55.0, 58.3, 44.4 and 61.0\%, then 68.8, 71.2, 65.1 and 78.8\%, then 52.9, 47.3, 58.1 and 60.0\%. A Spearman test of per-case detection against position reaches $\alpha = 0.05$ in one of the eight cells (NQ/Qwen, $\rho = -0.076$, $p = 0.048$), which does not survive correction over the eight tests ($p \approx 0.39$) and corresponds to almost no relationship in any case. The quartile figures fluctuate without a direction.

We therefore treat the prefix as exchangeable with the eligible set for the detection rates reported, which is what the intervals require. This checks position only. It does not rule out some other ordering effect in the datasets that happens not to show up as a monotone trend in detection.


\section{The markdown normalisation defect}
\label{app:markdown}

Sizing the defect before the fix, on 300 answers per cell, stripping markdown lowers Claude's whole-answer entailment score by 0.005 to 0.012 on Natural Questions and 0.021 to 0.043 on HotpotQA, moving it by more than 0.05 on 4.7 to 9.7\% and 17.7 to 19.3\% of answers respectively.
